# How we write and teach

The point is not to sound technical. The point is to make difficult things understandable without making them less accurate.

We speak to people who are curious about spatial data, people who use GIS every day, developers building spatial products, researchers and people who have arrived with one practical question. They should feel welcome before they know the terminology.

## Start with the reader's world

Lead with the question, task or outcome a person recognises.

Instead of opening with:

> A structured knowledge and implementation project for spatial analysis algorithms.

Try:

> Understand the methods behind the maps, models and decisions that shape our world.

Instead of:

> A curated sequence through canonical algorithm records.

Try:

> Find a way through the subject that makes sense for what you want to do.

Instead of:

> Reproducible implementations and benchmark protocols.

Try:

> Try the method, see what happens and compare how different approaches perform.

The technical term still belongs in the explanation. It just doesn't have to be the first thing a reader meets.

## Our voice

- **Clear:** use everyday words where they work; introduce specialist terms when they help.
- **Warm:** sound like a thoughtful person helping someone understand something, not an institution announcing a programme.
- **Confident:** explain what the reader will get. Don't inflate claims or hide uncertainty.
- **Curious:** invite people to test, compare, question and explore.
- **Practical:** connect ideas to real tasks, places, data and decisions.
- **Respectful:** don't talk down to beginners or bore experienced readers with unnecessary basics.

## Familiar on the surface, intelligent underneath

The experience should feel easy to enter. A reader should quickly know where to click, what they will find and what they can do next.

Behind that simplicity, the work can be rigorous: mathematics, source checking, implementation details, performance, edge cases and uncertainty. We do not remove the depth. We make it easier to reach.

## Use language people recognise

Prefer:

- "What you'll learn" over "learning outcomes" when speaking to readers.
- "Try it" over "implementation exercise" in a call to action.
- "Where this is useful" over "applications" in an introduction.
- "What can go wrong" over "failure modes" in a beginner-facing explanation.
- "Go deeper" over "advanced technical extension".
- "Help improve this" over "contribute to the knowledge lifecycle".

Keep exact technical language in the technical sections where precision matters. For example, explain what a coordinate reference system (CRS) is before using the abbreviation repeatedly.

## Don't do these things

- Don't make the reader decode internal project language.
- Don't invent trendy phrases or force slang into technical material.
- Don't call everything a journey, ecosystem, engine or framework.
- Don't promise mastery, effortless learning or guaranteed results.
- Don't use AI as the headline unless AI is genuinely the subject.
- Don't bury the useful answer under a long explanation of how the project is organised.
- Don't make every page sound like a course sales page.

## A useful page rhythm

1. **The familiar question:** what is the reader trying to understand or do?
2. **The simple answer:** explain the idea plainly.
3. **See it:** use a small example, visual, map or piece of code.
4. **What is happening underneath:** introduce the method and technical detail.
5. **Try it yourself:** provide an exercise or experiment.
6. **Go further:** show alternatives, limitations and references.
7. **Where to next:** link to a genuinely related topic.

Not every page needs every part. Use the structure when it helps, not as a formula to fill.

## Keep two voices in one project

**Reader-facing voice:** clear, warm, inviting and outcome-led. This is used on the homepage, learning paths, newsletter introductions, membership pages and contribution invitations.

**Technical voice:** precise, careful and explicit about assumptions, evidence, mathematical notation, software versions and limitations. This is used in algorithm records, implementation notes, benchmarks and research documentation.

They should sound like the same thoughtful project. The difference is how much precision the reader needs at that moment, not a different personality.

## Before publishing, ask

- Does the first paragraph tell someone why this matters?
- Would a person outside our team understand the headings?
- Have we explained specialist terms before relying on them?
- Is there a concrete example or next action?
- Have we kept the technical depth where it belongs?
- Have we said what we know, what we tested and what remains uncertain?
